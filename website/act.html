Take action in 10 minutes

The people who decide whether discriminatory 911 calls stay legal are your members of Congress, your state lawmakers and your city council. Here is how to reach all of them, plus a few other things that make a real difference.

  1. Call and write your lawmakers (2–5 minutes)

    Enter your address below. You get your two U.S. Senators, your U.S. Representative, and letters for your state lawmakers and city council. Calls count most, so each card has a call button and a 30-second script. Start now ↓

  2. Share your county’s numbers

    People act when they see their own county. Make a share image for your county.

  3. Make sure you can vote

    Lawmakers listen hardest to constituents who vote, and city councils, district attorneys and sheriffs are often elected by a few thousand votes.

  4. Know your rights, and share them

    If the police are called on you, or immigration agents come to your door, knowing your rights protects you and the people around you.

  5. Join the campaign in your state

    Volunteer to verify old laws, export CDC data, or organize locally, and suggest edits to the draft bill.

  6. Win it where you live

    Get your city council or county board on record, or pass a local CAREN law. The local action toolkit has a resolution, a model ordinance, testimony and a public records request ready to copy.

  7. Tell your story

    Has someone called the police on you, or did your family live through segregation or lose a neighborhood? Share it, or record an elder's story. Stories reach people statistics don't.

  8. Learn the history, and teach it

    Short, sourced lessons on how we got here, with quizzes, quests and badges. Start learning, then host a teach-in.

Contact your lawmakers

Your address goes only to the U.S. Census Bureau geocoder to find your districts. It is never sent to us or stored.

Tips that make your message count: say you are a constituent and give your ZIP code; keep it to one ask; your own experience matters more than statistics; be polite to staff, since they tally every contact. Members of Congress don't publish email addresses, so use the official contact form. New members of Congress take office January 3, 2027. Writing to them early, before their priorities are set, is especially useful.

These organizations publish free, up-to-date guides. We link to them rather than giving legal advice ourselves.

If you're stopped by police ↗ACLU guide, in several languages Immigrants' rights ↗ACLU: what to do if ICE comes to your home, work or car Red cards ↗Immigrant Legal Resource Center: printable rights cards in many languages Immigrant Defense Project ↗ICE raid guides and rights materials United We Dream ↗Rapid-response networks and reporting ICE activity National Immigration Law Center ↗Policy updates and know-your-rights resources NAACP Legal Defense Fund ↗Civil rights, policing and voting rights National Lawyers Guild ↗Legal observers and protest legal support
If someone calls the police on you to discriminate: stay calm and stay safe first. Afterward, write down what happened, the time and place, and any officer names or badge numbers, and save any video. Where a CAREN-style law exists (San Francisco, Oregon), you may be able to sue. Elsewhere, a civil rights organization or your local civilian police oversight board can advise you. Sharing what happened, in your own words, in your letters above is one of the most persuasive things you can do.
