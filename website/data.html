Data and Methodology

Every map layer is built from the original publisher's data. A GitHub Actions workflow reruns scripts/build_data.py every Monday. When a source publishes new data, the map updates automatically. Congressional membership and committee rosters load live in your browser each time you open the page.

Priority score

Each county gets a score from 0 to 1:

score = 0.30 × lynchings + 0.30 × police killings per capita + 0.15 × race/ethnicity hate crimes per capita + 0.25 × minority population share

  • Lynchings are log-scaled, so one county's extreme count doesn't flatten everything else.
  • Per-capita rates divide by population + 25,000. This keeps a small county with one incident from outranking a large county with a sustained pattern.
  • Each factor is capped at its 95th percentile and scaled to 0–1.
  • “High-overlap” counties (purple rings) are high on at least 3 of 4 factors:
    • lynchings at or above the median of counties with any;
    • police-killing rate at or above the 75th percentile, with at least 2 killings;
    • hate-crime rate at or above the 75th percentile, with at least 2 incidents;
    • minority share of 40% or more.

Worked examples

Each factor is scaled from 0 to 1, multiplied by its weight, and added up. The 95th-percentile caps currently fall at 14 lynching victims, 9.0 police killings per 100,000 residents and 17.7 race/ethnicity hate crimes per 100,000 residents.

Changing the weights

The weights are an advocacy judgment, not a statistical model. To change them, edit PRIORITY_WEIGHTS near build_priority() in scripts/build_data.py. Keep the four numbers adding up to 1. Then run python3 scripts/build_data.py and commit the updated website/data/counties.json, or wait for the Monday refresh. For example:

  • Focus on present-day policing: lynchings 0.15, police 0.40, hate crimes 0.20, people of color 0.25. Large cities with high police-killing rates rise; Deep South counties with old lynching records but little recent violence fall.
  • Focus on where people are most exposed: lynchings 0.20, police 0.25, hate crimes 0.15, people of color 0.40. Majority-minority counties rise everywhere, including the Southwest and Texas border counties.

The “high-overlap” rings don't depend on the weights. They count how many of the four factors are high on their own.

Caveats.
  • No national dataset tracks racially motivated 911 calls themselves. These layers are proxies for where such calls are likely to cause harm.
  • FBI hate-crime reporting is voluntary, and participation varies widely by agency.
  • Police-killing locations are geocoded by Mapping Police Violence.
  • EJI counts cover African American victims only, 1877–1950.
  • County-level choropleths use 2017-vintage county shapes, so Connecticut's new planning regions show as gray.

What “suspicious person” 911 calls lead to

No city publishes the race of the person a 911 caller reports. What cities do publish is what happened next. Most resident-initiated “suspicious person” calls end with officers finding nothing, or with no enforcement at all. Every one of those calls still sends armed officers to confront someone.

City (past 12 months)CallsArrest, citation or police reportNothing foundOther outcomes

Duplicate, cancelled and voided calls are excluded. “Nothing found” means gone on arrival, unable to locate, unfounded or no merit. “Other” includes advised, assistance rendered and “necessary action taken”. Call types differ by city: Seattle's category also covers suspicious vehicles and incidents, and New Orleans doesn't code arrests separately from reports. Map points are grouped into ~500 m cells, and cells with fewer than 3 calls are dropped.
