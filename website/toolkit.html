Local action toolkit

San Francisco passed its CAREN Act in about three months because one supervisor introduced it and residents showed up. These templates help you do the same: get your city or county on record for a federal CAREN Act, pass a local version, and gather the data that proves the need. Replace everything in [brackets].

Not legal advice. Have a local attorney or your council's legal staff review any ordinance before it is introduced. Volunteer legal reviewers can help: sign up or ask. The templates are in English because officials receive them in English.

The plan, in eight steps

  1. Map who decides. Find your council member and the committee that handles public safety. Use the lawmaker lookup.
  2. Gather the local evidence. Your county page has your county's history. File the records request below to get local 911 “suspicious person” call outcomes.
  3. Collect stories. Ask people who've had the police called on them to share, with consent, on the Stories page or at the meeting.
  4. Build a coalition. Churches, civil rights groups, neighborhood associations, unions, and Asian American, Latino and Native organizations. Use the sign-on letter below.
  5. Meet a council member's staff. Bring the resolution, one page of local data, and two people with stories. Use the meeting checklist.
  6. Ask for a sponsor. One council member introduces the resolution or ordinance.
  7. Pack the hearing. Sign people up for public comment with the testimony template, and get a letter to the editor published the week before.
  8. Follow up and report back. Thank the sponsor publicly, tell us on the quest board (“Win a local resolution,” 400 XP), and send the resolution to your members of Congress.

Public comment (about 2 minutes)

Public records request: 911 “suspicious person” calls

Most cities have to release call-for-service data. Each state's public records law has its own name, deadlines and fees; see the National Freedom of Information Coalition for your state, or file and track the request through MuckRock. If your city already publishes this data, tell us and we'll add it to the map.

Meeting with a lawmaker or their staff

  • Before: ask for 20 minutes. Bring 2–4 people, ideally constituents with different backgrounds, and at least one with a story.
  • Bring one page: the ask, three local numbers from your county page, the San Francisco and Oregon precedents, and a contact.
  • Make one ask: “Will you sponsor (or co-sponsor) this resolution?” Then stop talking and listen.
  • Listen for concerns: “people will be afraid to call 911” is the most common. Answer: the law protects good-faith calls and only applies with specific intent to discriminate.
  • After: send a thank-you within a day that repeats the ask and any promised follow-up. Record what they said for the coalition.
