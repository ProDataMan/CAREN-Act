Old Laws Tracker: Getting Jim Crow and Sundown Laws Off the Books

Courts can't enforce segregation and sundown laws anymore, but some of their text is still printed in official city codes, county codes, and state constitutions. Repealing that text is a public statement that it was wrong. Boynton Beach, Florida did exactly that in 2024.

How repeal actually works. Only the body that passed a law can repeal it: a city council, a county commission, or a state legislature. State constitutional text also needs a ballot vote. A court can't strike unenforced text from a code, and lawsuits over laws nobody enforces are usually dismissed because no one has been currently harmed. So this tracker doesn't file legal motions. It builds a verified, cited case for each law and hands the right official a ready-to-adopt repeal ordinance or bill. A real constituent or an allied official delivers it, and the tracker follows up until the law is gone.

The process

  1. ReportAnyone submits the citation, the exact text, and a link to the current code.
  2. VerifyAn editor confirms the text is still in force in the official code.
  3. DraftThe site generates a repeal ordinance or bill and a cover letter.
  4. DeliverA local resident or an ally's office sends it to the body that can repeal it.
  5. Follow upEvery 30 days the tracker reminds the volunteer who took the case to check on it, until the law is repealed.

Where to look next

  • On the Books (UNC-Chapel Hill): a machine-learning search that found about 2,000 Jim Crow laws in North Carolina session laws from 1866–1967, plus a companion South Carolina project. Check each one against the current General Statutes.
  • Municipal code libraries (Municode, American Legal, eCode360) for cities that adopted sundown or segregation ordinances. The Sundown Towns database lists candidate towns.
  • State constitutions: several have removed segregation-era language by ballot vote, for example Alabama's 2022 recompiled constitution. Others still contain provisions that need review.
  • Racially restrictive housing covenants in property records (see Mapping Prejudice). Many states now let owners strike them.
  • State law revision commissions and code revisors' offices: they handle “obsolete law” cleanup bills and can carry several repeals at once.
