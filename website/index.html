How to read the map

The heat map

Every U.S. county gets a priority score from 0 to 1 that combines four things:

  • its history of racial terror lynching (30% of the score);
  • its rate of police killings per resident (30%);
  • its rate of race-motivated hate crimes per resident (15%);
  • its share of residents who are people of color (25%).

Each county with a score above 0.15 (about 2,000 of 3,222) adds a glow centered on that county.

  • Color runs from blue (lower) to red (higher). The glow is weighted by the score squared, so high-scoring counties stand out much more than middling ones.
  • Glows add up. A deep red area means several neighboring counties all score high, as in the Black Belt from Mississippi to Georgia. One high-scoring county surrounded by low ones shows as a smaller orange spot. So red marks regional patterns, not single counties.
  • Zoom changes the picture. At the national view, nearby counties blend together. Zoom in and they separate, so you can see which counties drive a hot spot.
  • For exact numbers, use the purple rings or the county layer. Purple rings mark the 104 counties that are high on at least 3 of the 4 factors; a thick ring means all 4. Click a ring, or turn on Minority population share and click any county, to see its score and the numbers behind it. Each pop-up links to a shareable county page.

The heat map is a way to prioritize outreach: where the harm of discriminatory police calls is likely to land hardest. It is not a measure of how many such calls happen. No national data on that exists.

  • Where to organize: hot spots, members of Congress on the Judiciary Committees (who would hear the bill), and old segregation laws still on the books.

Reading a county

Example: Hinds County, Mississippi (Jackson) ranks #2 of 3,222 counties with a score of 0.83:

  • 22 documented lynching victims, which hits the maximum for that factor;
  • 21 people killed by police since 2013, among the highest rates per resident in the country;
  • 77% of residents are people of color.

Its hate-crime rate is moderate, so 3 of its 4 factors are high. By contrast, Cook County, Illinois (Chicago) has far more police killings in total (165), but per resident its rate is about a third of Hinds County's, so it ranks #484. Rates matter more than raw counts. The data page walks through the math.
