How the San Francisco CAREN Act Came About

⬇ Download the original ordinance (PDF)

San Francisco Ordinance No. 219-20, File No. 200735, adding Police Code §637. Official source: sfbos.org.

The incidents that prompted it

San Francisco Supervisor Shamann Walton introduced the ordinance in early July 2020. He was responding to a run of widely shared videos showing white callers phoning police about Black and brown people who were doing ordinary things. Two incidents were cited most often:

  • Pacific Heights, San Francisco (June 2020). A white couple confronted James Juanillo, a Filipino American man, as he chalked “Black Lives Matter” on the retaining wall of his own home, and called the police on him. The video spread quickly and gave the proposal its local urgency.
  • Central Park, New York (May 25, 2020). Amy Cooper called 911 on Christian Cooper (no relation), a Black birdwatcher who had asked her to leash her dog. On the call she said “an African American man” was threatening her life. It happened the same day George Floyd was killed in Minneapolis, and it became the best-known example of a weaponized 911 call.

These followed earlier Bay Area cases that drew national attention in 2018, such as the calls made about a Black family barbecuing at Oakland’s Lake Merritt and about an 8-year-old Black girl selling water in San Francisco.

The name is a deliberate backronym. Caution Against Racial and Exploitative Non-Emergencies spells CAREN, a play on “Karen,” the slang term then used for this kind of caller.

The core idea: make the caller, not the person reported, bear the cost. The ordinance gives the targeted person a civil cause of action against the caller, with damages of at least $1,000 plus attorneys’ fees and costs, and punitive damages in a proper case. It creates no new crime.

What the ordinance actually says

§637(b) makes it unlawful to knowingly cause a peace officer to arrive at a location to contact a person, with the specific intent to act on the basis of that person’s actual or perceived race, color, ancestry, ethnicity, national origin, place of birth, sex, age, religion, creed, disability, sexual orientation, gender identity, weight, or height. The intent must be to do one of the following:

  1. infringe the person’s rights under the California or U.S. Constitution;
  2. discriminate against the person;
  3. cause the person to feel harassed, humiliated, or embarrassed;
  4. cause the person to be expelled from a place where they are lawfully located;
  5. damage the person’s reputation or standing in the community; or
  6. damage the person’s financial, economic, consumer, or business prospects.

Section 1(c) of the findings says the ordinance “is not intended to discourage individuals from contacting law enforcement when they are facing real danger or desire to report a crime.” In the SF text, that good-faith principle sits in the findings only. The draft federal bill turns it into an explicit statutory defense (Section 5).

Why a federal version?

The SF ordinance only protects people inside San Francisco, and state laws differ widely in scope and remedy. A federal statute would give every person in the country the same baseline remedy, while leaving stronger state and local laws in place.
