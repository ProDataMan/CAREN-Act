Policy brief · Federal CAREN Act

Make it illegal to use the police as a weapon of discrimination

The problem. Across the country, people have called the police on Black, Latino, Asian, Native and other neighbors for barbecuing, birdwatching, selling water, waiting in a coffee shop, or canvassing their own district. These calls send armed officers to confront people who are doing nothing wrong. They put lives at risk, humiliate and endanger the people targeted, and waste emergency resources. In most of the country, the person targeted has no remedy against the caller.

~3×Black residents who had police contact were about this many times as likely as white residents to face threats or force (BJS, 2020)
11–0San Francisco's Board of Supervisors passed the CAREN Act unanimously in 2020

Precedents

  • San Francisco (2020): CAREN Act, Police Code §637. Civil remedy of at least $1,000.
  • Oregon (2019): civil action against a person who calls police with racial bias. Authored by then-state Rep. Janelle Bynum, now a U.S. Representative.
  • New York (2020): civil action for false reports to police based on race or other protected traits.
  • California AB 1775 (2020): stronger penalties for false, bias-based reports.

What the bill does

  • Makes it unlawful to knowingly cause officers to contact someone with the specific intent to discriminate on the basis of race or another protected trait, or to harass or expel them.
  • Gives the person harmed a civil action: at least $1,000 ($500 from the caller, $500 from the responding agency), attorneys' fees, and punitive damages for malicious or reckless violations.
  • Asks the Attorney General to study national data on bias-motivated emergency calls.

What it does not do

  • It protects good-faith calls: an explicit defense for anyone reporting an actual or reasonably suspected crime, emergency or threat.
  • It creates no new crime and no new police powers.
  • It does not preempt stronger state and local laws.

Why federal action

Local and state laws protect only people who happen to live there. A federal floor gives every person in the country the same remedy and sends a national message: calling 911 is for emergencies, not for enforcing prejudice.

The ask: introduce or cosponsor a federal CAREN Act; request a Judiciary Committee hearing on bias-motivated police calls; or include these protections in a broader civil rights or policing bill.
