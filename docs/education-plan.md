# Education plan: how we got here, and the way out

**Status:** Phase 1 and part of Phase 4 are built (2026-11-09). Modules 1, 5, 8 and 10 are drafted, the others are outlined. Progress, XP, badges and state teams work. The open questions below have been answered. The content lives in `website/data/learn.json`.

## What this adds

A "Learn" part of the site that explains where systemic racism came from, using primary sources, data and the map we already have. It also connects every lesson to something a reader can do. The CAREN Act stays the main goal. The history explains why the bill is needed, and the actions are how readers help pass it.

## Ground rules

These keep the project credible. Lawmakers, reporters and opponents will check our sources, and one weak claim can discredit a hundred strong ones.

1. **Every claim gets an evidence label.**
   - **Documented:** primary sources or official records. Examples are the Tuskegee study, the Virginia slave codes, and court-ordered sterilizations.
   - **Debated:** facts are documented but historians disagree about what they mean. An example is Margaret Sanger's motives in the 1939 "Negro Project".
   - **Belief or myth:** a religious, folk or conspiracy story. We explain it, say who holds it and why, and present it as a belief, not as history.
2. **Cite the original wherever possible.** That means the law, court record, government report or archive, with a reputable secondary source next to it.
3. **Racism is treated as a system built by people for reasons we can name:** land, labor, profit, political power and fear of revolt. It is not presented as a fixed trait of any group. Every module also tells the story of the people who resisted it, including white abolitionists and allies.
4. **A historian or subject expert reviews a module before it goes live.** We recruit them through the volunteer page, using a new "historian / reviewer" skill.

## The central question: "Why the fear?"

Historians' answer is not a forgotten ancient secret. The evidence points the other way, in four ways.

- **The ancient world did not divide people by skin color the way we do.** Greeks, Romans and Egyptians had slavery, but it was based on conquest and debt, not color. Frank Snowden's *Before Color Prejudice* (1983) documents this.
- **Modern race was invented in roughly 1450–1700 to justify taking land and people.** The steps are well documented:
  - papal bulls (*Dum Diversas* 1452, *Romanus Pontifex* 1455, *Inter caetera* 1493), which the Vatican repudiated in 2023;
  - Virginia's 1662 law making a child's status follow the mother;
  - the 1705 Virginia slave codes;
  - France's *Code Noir* (1685).
- **The ideas came after the policies.** Ibram X. Kendi (*Stamped from the Beginning*) and Edmund Morgan (*American Slavery, American Freedom*) show that racist ideas were developed to defend profitable practices already in place. After Bacon's Rebellion (1676), Virginia's laws deliberately split poor white and Black workers apart.
- **The fear was real, but it was fear of losing power and of retribution.** Examples:
  - the Haitian Revolution (1791–1804) and Nat Turner's rebellion (1831);
  - the Black voting majorities in South Carolina and Mississippi during Reconstruction;
  - today's "replacement" rhetoric, which goes back to Madison Grant's *The Passing of the Great Race* (1916).

Religious texts were used too, and the module shows exactly how:

- **The "Curse of Ham":** Genesis 9 curses Canaan, not Ham, and says nothing about skin color. The racial reading was added centuries later and became a standard pro-slavery argument in the U.S.
- **The 1807 "Slave Bible":** it removed most passages about freedom, including the Exodus story.
- **The Book of Enoch (1 Enoch):** canonical in the Ethiopian Orthodox Church and quoted in the Letter of Jude. It tells of the Watchers and the Nephilim and contains no racial teaching. We cover it because modern groups have built race myths around Genesis 6 and Enoch, labeled as beliefs:
  - Christian Identity's "two-seedline" theory, a white-supremacist doctrine;
  - the Nation of Islam's Yakub story;
  - some Hebrew Israelite teachings.

  Knowing where these come from protects readers from them.

## Reproductive control: what the evidence shows

This is the topic that prompted the plan, so it gets the most careful treatment.

**Documented abuses:**
- More than 60,000 Americans were sterilized under eugenics laws in 32 states. After the 1950s, Black women were targeted heavily. North Carolina's Eugenics Board alone sterilized about 7,600 people, and the state paid survivors compensation in 2014.
- The 1973 sterilization of the Relf sisters in Montgomery, Alabama, ages 12 and 14.
- Fannie Lou Hamer's "Mississippi appendectomy": she was sterilized without her consent.
- A 1976 government audit (GAO) found thousands of sterilizations of Native women by the Indian Health Service.
- Mass sterilization in Puerto Rico. By 1965, about a third of mothers had been sterilized.
- Sterilizations of women in California prisons, 2006–2010, confirmed by a state audit in 2014.

**Debated:**
- **Margaret Sanger's motives.** Sanger supported eugenics. Planned Parenthood's 1939 "Negro Project" was run by white leaders, although W.E.B. Du Bois supported it. Planned Parenthood itself removed Sanger's name from its Manhattan clinic in 2020 over her eugenics ties. Her 1939 letter saying "we do not want word to go out that we want to exterminate the Negro population" is real. Historians disagree about what it meant: most read it as worry that rumors would spread, not as a plan.
- **"Founded to exterminate Black Americans."** The evidence does not show this. Sanger opposed abortion in her lifetime, and Planned Parenthood did not provide abortions until about 1970. We present the claim, where it comes from, and what the record shows.

**The data:**
- The CDC's annual abortion report shows much higher abortion rates among Black women.
- Research (Guttmacher Institute, CDC) links the gap to higher unintended pregnancy rates, which follow from unequal access to contraception and health care, from poverty, and from distrust of medicine.
- The page will show the CDC figures together with those causes, so the numbers aren't read as proof of a plot. We also show Black maternal mortality, which is about three times the white rate (CDC). That is the same unequal health system at work.

## Proposed additional modules

Ranked by how directly they support the campaign:

1. **The evidence of equal or better performance:**
   - résumé audit studies (identical résumés with "Black-sounding" names get fewer callbacks; Bertrand & Mullainathan 2004 and later field experiments);
   - Black women as the most educated group by enrollment;
   - HBCUs.

   This answers "we perform as well or better" with data.
2. **Voting rights and disenfranchisement:** poll taxes and literacy tests, the Voting Rights Act, *Shelby County v. Holder* (2013), felony disenfranchisement, and redistricting.
3. **Mass incarceration and the war on drugs:** the 100:1 crack-to-powder sentencing gap (18:1 since 2010), and the 13th Amendment exception today.
4. **Wealth was built and blocked:**
   - New Deal exclusions (Social Security first left out farm and domestic workers);
   - the GI Bill in practice;
   - redlining to subprime lending;
   - the racial wealth gap.
5. **Environmental racism:** Cancer Alley in Louisiana, Flint, freeways and pollution, and heat islands in formerly redlined areas.
6. **Black towns and self-determination:** Eatonville, Allensworth, Nicodemus, Mound Bayou, Greenwood before 1921, Durham's Parrish Street. These are the stories of what was built, not only what was destroyed.
7. **How movements won:** the Montgomery bus boycott, the Civil Rights Act and Voting Rights Act campaigns, and the organizing, litigation and lobbying behind them. These are tactics for passing the CAREN Act.
8. **Surveillance and repression:** COINTELPRO and the targeting of King and the Black Panthers, and modern surveillance of protests.
9. **Violence against Latino, Asian and Native communities:** lynchings of Mexican Americans (about 547 from 1848 to 1928, Carrigan & Webb), Vincent Chin (1982), anti-Asian hate in 2020, and missing and murdered Indigenous women.
10. **Media and image-making:** *The Birth of a Nation* (1915) and the revived Klan, minstrelsy, and crime-news framing.
11. **Black veterans:** Isaac Woodard (1946), and veterans lynched in uniform after World War I. Tulsa's defenders were veterans.

## Making it a game

The game rewards learning and real action, never arguing or shaming.

- **Paths and badges:** finishing a module earns a badge. Finishing three modules plus one quest earns "Ally, level 1", and so on.
- **Quests are real tasks:** verifying an old law, exporting CDC data, calling a lawmaker, hosting a teach-in, or getting a city council to pass a resolution. Many already exist on the site.
  - A quest is checked by its result where possible, for example the old-law report or the volunteer record. Otherwise people report it themselves.
  - The high-value quests carry the most points: verified laws, a teach-in, or a council resolution.
- **Team scores by state, not individual leaderboards.** That prevents toxic competition, and the state teams match how laws get passed.
- **"Source detective":** a recurring quiz where players label claims as documented, debated or myth. It trains people to resist misinformation from every side.
- **Where progress is saved:**
  - in the browser, with no account needed;
  - in Azure SQL for people who sign in, so progress follows them between devices and state team totals can be counted. It needs a new `progress` table.
  - Personal data stays private, like volunteer sign-ups.
- **Gold stars** stay tied to accepted bill edits, as they are now.
